# The Truth About Gaming Audiences - Part 2: Ad-Avoidance Is a Cohort Not a Channel

Part 1 established that gaming audiences are reachable everywhere a conventional channel operates, and that the only unaddressed context is in-environment. The strongest pushback to that claim deserves to be stated at full strength before it is examined: reachable is not the same as receptive. 

This audience ignores paid media. It runs ad blockers at rates other segments don't. Serving an impression to a person who has structurally opted out of impressions is reach in name only. The avoidance is real, and no honest account of this audience can begin anywhere else.

Granting the observation fully is the point of this piece, because the observation is accurate and the conclusion drawn from it is not. The conclusion the market draws is that the avoidance is a property of gaming — that something about the hobby produces an audience uniquely resistant to advertising, and that this resistance is what justifies specialist access at a premium. The avoidance is real; its attribution to gaming is the error.

## The cause is the cohort
The ad-avoidance attributed to gamers tracks a demographic cohort: younger, more digitally native, higher-income. Ad-blocker adoption, paid-media skepticism, and subscription-over-advertising preferences are population-level patterns that concentrate in exactly that cohort, and they concentrate there whether or not the person in question has ever touched a game. 

Gaming over-indexes on this cohort — heavily. As a result, the avoidance shows up wherever gaming audiences are measured, and the hobby gets credited with a trait the demographics brought with them. The same population avoids ads identically whether or not you tag them gamers.

The precise form of the claim matters, because the imprecise form is false. It is not the case that gamers are no more ad-avoidant than the general population — they are. But they are more avoidant because of who they are demographically, not because of what they play. Remove the gaming tag from this population and the avoidance remains, at the same intensity, for the same reasons. Add the gaming tag to an older, less digitally native population and the avoidance does not appear. The avoidance correlates with the cohort, not the interest.

The counterfactual is what makes the distinction load-bearing rather than academic. If the avoidance were endemic to gaming — if the hobby itself produced the resistance — then "gaming audiences are uniquely hard to reach" would be true, and the endemic premium built on it would be justified. That is exactly what does not hold. 

Gaming confers no special elusiveness on anyone; it correlates with a population-level trend that has nothing to do with video games. If the premium's premise were true, the premium would be earned. The premise is what fails.

## The strongest card, turned around
Elusiveness is the strongest card in the endemic deck, and stating it accurately does not weaken it — it redirects it. Taken seriously, the observation says this: a growing cohort has structurally opted out of interruptive advertising in the channels where reach is already solved. 

That is not an argument for spending more in those channels under a gaming label. Pouring additional spend into feeds and pre-roll to chase a cohort that has opted out of feeds and pre-roll is paying a premium to intensify the thing being avoided. Stated accurately, the elusiveness argument is an argument against more of the same reach.

What the observation argues for is the thing Part 1 identified: the one context this cohort occupies that has never been addressable at all. The avoidance is directed at a format experience — interruption in conventional channels — not at brands as such. 

A context where the cohort is present by choice, and where no interruptive history exists, is precisely where an avoidance-shaped audience would need to be met. The elusiveness card, played face up, points at the unaddressed context. The correct response to a cohort that avoids conventional reach is not more conventional reach with a gaming tag on it.

What this piece has not said is that meeting the cohort in that context is simply a matter of showing up with the right formats. Whether the formats fit — and what that fit does and does not establish — is Part 3.
