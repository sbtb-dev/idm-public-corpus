# Gaming's Attention Premium Has No Buyer

Endemic game studios already ran the attention-quality test. The verdict is written in their media plans.
***

Buried in EMARKETER's forecast commentary on US game advertising (see Sources) is a sentence that settles an argument the gaming ad market has been having with itself for a decade. The forecaster notes that most in-game ad spend runs on mobile — and that most of those ads are bought by game developers themselves. Read it again. The gaming ad category's majority customer is the game industry. The demand the category keeps waiting for is already inside the category, at scale, allocated. It is simply not buying what the category's sales narrative sells.

That narrative is the attention-quality thesis. It holds that game environments deliver superior attention — longer dwell, fewer competing stimuli, less avoidance — and that this attention converts to better outcomes, which justifies a premium and predicts that gaming's ad dollars will one day match gaming's share of consumer time. Every version of the pitch ends the same way: the gap between time spent and dollars earned is the opportunity.

The thesis has a testable prediction, and it has always had one buyer uniquely equipped to test it. That buyer has been running the test continuously for over a decade. This piece reads the result.

## The buyer who never had to take attention on faith
Attention measurement exists because most advertisers cannot observe what happens after exposure. A brand buys attention metrics as a leading indicator — a proxy sold on the promise that it predicts outcomes nobody can watch directly. The entire attention industry is built on that gap between the ad and the result.

A game operator has no such gap. A studio acquiring players observes install, activation, day-one retention, day-seven, day-thirty, session depth, first purchase, and lifetime value — resolved by acquisition source, inside its own instrumentation, as a condition of operating. The endemic buyer holds the ground truth that attention metrics were invented to approximate. When a studio wants to know whether one channel's users outperform another's, it does not commission a brand-lift study. It reads its own funnel.

And this buyer spends. AppsFlyer's State of Gaming for Marketers puts global mobile gaming user-acquisition spend at $25 billion in 2025, roughly half of it in the US, across a measured dataset of 24.8 billion installs — 14.1 billion of them paid. That is not a test budget. It is one of the largest, most continuously optimized pools of performance spend in advertising, reallocated in days by teams with no channel loyalty and full outcome visibility.

So the conditions for the experiment are complete. A buyer with ground-truth outcome data, enormous budgets, ruthless reallocation discipline, and native fluency in game environments has been choosing between ad formats inside/around/beyond those environments for years. If gaming-context attention converted to outcome at a premium, this buyer would have found it first, funded it hardest, and never needed to be educated about it.

## The allocation is the publication
Here is what that buyer does. Endemic studios buy in-game inventory constantly — and the formats they fund are rewarded video, interstitials, and playables: interruptive, incentivized, conversion-proximate units bought through AppLovin, Unity, and Meta and reconciled against installs in a mobile measurement partner. AppLovin alone — a platform whose demand remains overwhelmingly gaming — generated $3.22 billion in advertising revenue in 2024 and scaled past a $1.6 billion quarterly run-rate by late 2025. One performance vendor's gaming-driven ad business is several times the size of the entire US in-game brand advertising market.

Now hold that spending against the attention thesis's own rankings. Attention frameworks rate intrinsic, non-interruptive, in-world placement highest — the billboard in the stadium, the branded object in the scene, the format that shares the frame with gameplay. They rate the interruption lowest. The endemic buyer funds the interruption at billions and leaves the intrinsic unit nearly untouched. Same venue. Same audience. Same buyer. Full outcome visibility. The buyer who can see the result allocates against the attention hierarchy, not with it.

That is not an absence of evidence. It is a result. Thousands of incrementality tests, geo holdouts, and cohort comparisons have been run against this question by the best-equipped analysts in advertising, and the finding was never published as a study because it was published as an allocation. The money is the paper.

## The detector demonstrably works
The standard defense of the intrinsic format's stagnation is friction: no standard unit, fragmented supply, measurement gaps, operational cost. The defense fails against a control case, because this exact buyer population recently adopted a gaming-context format with worse friction on every axis — and adopted it fast.

Creator marketing had no standard unit, no comparison set, no third-party verification, endemic fraud problems, bespoke production per placement, and supply fragmented across tens of thousands of independent operators. It had no IAB framework, no MRC accreditation, no viewability standard. By every friction argument offered for intrinsic in-game's stagnation, creator marketing should never have scaled.

It scaled anyway, and the record is quantitative. Influencer marketing in games grew from $822 million in 2019 to $2.5 billion in 2021 to over $4.4 billion in 2022, per figures originating with Business Insider Intelligence — roughly a fivefold expansion in three years. By 2025 the practice had graduated from awareness play to performance channel: industry analysis describes creator campaigns competing for the same budget line as Meta and Google, and among top mobile RPG and MMORPG titles, around 60% now rely heavily on creator campaigns. And the adoption ran ahead of the measurement — studios scaled creator spend on the evidence of their own telemetry, watching wishlist spikes and store-rank movement, before any attribution standard existed to bless it.

That is the calibration reading. The endemic loop found creator marketing and scaled it fivefold in three years, through maximal friction, on internal evidence alone. The same loop has been pointed at intrinsic in-game advertising for longer, under better conditions — programmatic delivery, standardized units, IAB and MRC intrinsic measurement guidelines, accredited verification — and has found nothing worth scaling. A detector that fires that fast on a true positive is not missing this one. It is returning null.

The objection writes itself: creator marketing scaled because it is trackable — codes, links, attributable installs — not because the attention was better. Grant it in full, because the objection is the thesis. If detectable outcome is what governs endemic adoption, then endemic allocation is decided by attributable results and not by attention quality — which is precisely the finding. The objection and the piece agree.

## The price already reflects the verdict
The demand side's verdict has a supply-side twin, and an independent authority filed it in June 2026. Jounce Media — the supply-path analysts programmatic buyers use to read the RTB market — named desktop apps a distinct ad environment for the first time. These are the gaming companion tools running on-screen across titles like Minecraft and Fortnite, and Overwolf operates 215 of the 241 apps Jounce identified. Jounce found the category now accounts for 11% of all web bid requests — a larger single supply source than Yahoo, Raptive, or Mediavine.

Read what the market did with that supply. It benchmarked Overwolf against Raptive and Mediavine — web display ad-representation networks. The buyer safeguards Jounce specified are app identification, refresh tied to pixels in view, and accredited viewability verification. Every safeguard measures whether an impression happened. The most gaming-native supply source on the open web is priced and graded as web display, with no gaming premium applied and none asked for.

That flat price is the attention thesis's second falsification, because the thesis predicts a gradient. If gaming-context attention carried a premium, price should ladder by proximity to play: intrinsic in-world placement at the top, the in-session overlay below it, adjacent web at the bottom. The market prices all three rungs as display. A thesis whose central prediction is a gradient is falsified by a flat line — and this flat line was drawn by supply-path analysts with no position in the category's self-image.

eMarketer's own forecast closes the loop from the brand side: US game ad revenues reach $10 billion only by 2029, in-game's share of digital ad spend sits at 2.3% in 2026 — and declines to 2% by 2029. The share is shrinking while the category grows. A market being slowly discovered does not lose share. A market that has been discovered, priced, and filed does.

## What would overturn this
The finding is falsifiable, and the falsification is welcome. One credible endemic result — a studio's own cohort data showing that players acquired or influenced through intrinsic in-game exposure outperform, at a margin that survives the unit's cost — would damage this argument. Anyone holding that evidence should publish it, because it is the proof the category has needed for fifteen years and has never produced.

But the counterexample cannot arrive as a quiet case study, because that is not what a positive result looks like in this market. Creator marketing showed the shape. When the endemic loop finds something, the finding becomes a line item, then a hire, then a team, then an agency category, then a visible shift in network mix — within a few years, in public, at billions. A true positive in this buyer population does not stay anecdotal. If the intrinsic result existed, it would already be an endemic practice, and the practice would be as visible as creator marketing became. The absence of the practice is the absence of the result.

Until then, the two findings stand together. Per-impression attention in game environments is real — grant every study that measures it. And the buyer who could check whether that attention converts to outcome at a premium has checked, continuously, with the best instrumentation in advertising, and priced the answer into a decade of allocation. Attention is a property of the format. The premium was a claim about the brand's result. The one buyer who could see the result declined to pay it.

One line has to stay clean at the close. This verdict covers the exposure unit and the attention premium attached to it. It says nothing about brand-attributed response evidence captured in-environment — the unit that has never been offered, never been priced, and never had a line in a plan. A unit that has never been quoted cannot have been rejected. What the endemic record proves is narrower and harder: the attention story was tested by the only buyer who could test it, and the dollars came back with the answer.
