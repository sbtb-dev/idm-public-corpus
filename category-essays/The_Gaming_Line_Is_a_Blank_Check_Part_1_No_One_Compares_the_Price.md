# The Gaming Line Is a Blank Check, Part 1: No One Compares the Price

*A gaming line can track cost and set a benchmark, and still never compare a gaming buy with the same unit outside gaming.*

---

The push to put gaming in the media plan is now loud and steady. The IAB's business case for gaming, published in August 2026, tells CMOs that gaming "has earned a place in your marketing plan" (IAB, p. 11). Gaming vendors make the case in stronger words. Overwolf asks for gaming's "seat next to TV, social and search in every media plan" (see sources).

Much of the case is right. The audience is large, people spend real time in game environments, and many gaming formats work. The problem is not the audience or the formats. It is the structure the case asks brands to build around them: a gaming line. This is an error in how the line is defined, not an error by the people who make the case for it.

## What a gaming line is

A gaming line is a line in the plan that anything with the gaming label can enter. An esports sponsorship qualifies. So does a branded in-game integration, a gaming creator buy, in-game display, a mobile playable, and a placement in a retailer's gaming hub.

A line holds the budget, has one owner, and has one benchmark that decides the next dollar. The IAB says gaming is "not one media channel" (IAB, p. 2). It also says that once gaming belongs in the plan, "it should have an owner, a business role, a measurement plan, and a path to scale decision" (IAB, p. 15). That is a line, whatever it is called.

## Checked on results, blank on comparison

A gaming line can be well controlled and still miss the one check that controls price. The IAB's case shows this, because its controls are real. It tests a gaming recommendation on business fit, audience, brand contribution, trust, and measurement. It asks for decision-ready metrics, a decision rule set before launch, and a next-dollar threshold. It lets the CMO say no for now. It lists cost metrics: CPM, cost per conversion, and cost per action. Its minimum test standard asks for a benchmark (IAB, pp. 14, 17).

What's missing is the comparison. Nothing in the August 2026 case tells the team to compare a gaming buy's cost with the same unit outside gaming. That is fair for a document written for CMOs, which leaves buying detail to the team. But its planners' companion, where that detail lives, also asks for a benchmark without naming one.

So a team can meet every standard with a benchmark drawn from other gaming buys. Its checks would show whether gaming worked, against gaming's own bar. They would not show whether the brand paid more for a unit than it pays for the same unit in its native line: the line where the brand buys that unit from other sellers.

That is the blank check. It is not blank on results or on cost metrics. It is blank on the comparison. Cost discipline rests on a basic rule: compare each unit with the same unit elsewhere. A line defined by the gaming label cannot apply that rule by itself.

## The condition doesn't need a label

The strongest argument for a gaming line is that gaming can serve any objective. The IAB puts it this way: "The same ecosystem can support reach, consideration, conversion, loyalty, and brand equity when the environment, format, and metrics are matched to the business goal" (IAB, p. 11). It tells CMOs not to ask whether gaming is upper- or lower-funnel, but which objective the plan should achieve.

At the level of formats, that is sound planning. Define the objective, then choose the format and the metric. This essay does not dispute it.

But the claim is conditional, and it is the format, not the gaming label, that meets the condition. The same document's format table files rewarded and intrinsic formats as display, video, or audio, measured on those lines' metrics (IAB, p. 20). When the condition holds, the format serves the objective, and the format already has a line.

A line is useful only when being in it predicts something: the unit, the terms, and what the buy returns. The gaming tag on a rewarded-video buy adds no unit or measure that "rewarded video" has not already set.

Yet the decision the case frames is about gaming as a whole: whether gaming deserves a defined role in the plan, one gaming recommendation, and a next-dollar threshold for gaming. It asks that gaming get "the same rigor they apply to social, CTV, retail media, audio, creator, and other major marketing channels" (IAB, p. 4). Gaming can be a marketing discipline without being a media channel. A line of budget does not split so easily.

For example, rewarded video is both a gaming format and a video format. A plan can tag it both ways. But its budget answers to one owner and one benchmark. If that benchmark is gaming's, the video owner does not see the buy, and no owner is assigned to compare it with the video line's other units on completed views.

## The comparison the line does not make

Established lines apply the rule as routine. Video is compared with video, display with display, a retailer's placement with that retailer's other placements and with other retailers' units. That is the rigor the case asks gaming to match.

A format that enters through a gaming line can skip that step, because its owner answers to gaming's benchmark and not to its native line's. Inside the line, rewarded video answers to a gaming bar, not to the video line's completed views. A placement in a retailer's gaming hub answers to a gaming bar, not to that retailer's other onsite units. Some items in the line share a unit: two in-game display buys can be compared with each other. But the line as a whole has no common unit, terms, or measure of return. It cannot set one benchmark that compares its items with each other, or with the same units outside it. Any price can be the line's price.

Not every gaming buy skips the step. A unit bought through the same pipes, on its native line's terms and with no gaming label, is compared the usual way. The gap is the buy that enters through a gaming recommendation and answers to its benchmark.

A gaming owner could close the gap from inside: require each format's benchmark to be its native line's unit. That works. But then the native line sets the price test, and the gaming line holds only a tag. That is the fix this series proposes, under another name. In effect, the gaming line's cost control is to undo the gaming line.

## What replaces it

The fix is not new. Each standard format sold as gaming goes back to its native line. There, it is compared with the same unit from other sellers, and judged the way that line judges its other buys. Some gaming buys are not a standard unit at all; a brand's role built inside a game is one. Part 2 sets out the rule for those and for the rest: when a line defined by environment earns its place, why a narrower gaming line only hides the same failure, and how a brand tests gaming supply without a gaming framework.
