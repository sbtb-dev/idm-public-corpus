# Immersive Media's Channel Problem

Immersive media has a channel problem. This piece locates the problem: Immersive media formats are incorrectly grouped under the label "gaming." It corrects the problem by clarifying a shared evidence standard across the channel. Without one, immersive media can't be planned against a common unit. The gaming label does the work the standard should do, and the work it does is wrong.

"Gaming" names an audience interest. It does not name a property the media formats grouped under the label share, because they do not share one. Display in a mobile title, a sponsored gaming stream, an esports activation, and a branded in-game integration produce different evidence of consumer behavior, on different terms, captured by different infrastructure. The only thing they hold in common is the audience spend targets. Grouping them by that audience label produces a media bundle - in name only.

That's the structural error. The most visible consequence is confusion between an immersive environment and the display signal running inside it. The venue and the signal are different things. 

## In-game media confusion
Immersive media's advertising boundary, stated plainly, is a single question. 

Does this environment produce direct behavioral evidence of consumer response at pre-conversion funnel stages, captured by native instrumentation, observable within a unified environment? 

Inferred, stated, or reconstructed evidence does not qualify. Platform level analytics do not qualify. Only direct behavioral evidence, attributable to the brand's presence inside the qualifying environment, meets the test. 

A game environment is capable of passing the test and qualifying as immersive media. It has the capacity to answer "yes" to the advertising boundary test. The qualification means brands can leverage it as an immersive media channel. 

A display ad in the same environment cannot pass the test — it is only capable of producing a viewability count. The impression is a display advertising unit — it is not immersive media. The game and the in-game ad share the same virtual space and belong to different channels. A planner working from the gaming label sees one thing where there are two.

Without the advertising boundary test, media planners treat in-game brand presence as a singular gaming channel, and conclude the environment offers nothing more than impressions and/or impression adjacent evidence. The conclusion is false. But nothing in the gaming bundle's vocabulary can correct it, because the bundle never defined a boundary to begin with.

The immersive media boundary dissolves the confusion and the gaming bundle, with it. What follows is the cost of operating without it. 

Three activations, each grouped under "gaming," each belonging to a different channel, none producing the evidence the immersive channel is defined by. The campaigns are real and recent; one industry account groups all three as instances of a single "gaming" strategy. Immersive media's advertising boundary test clarifies them as three different channels wearing one label.

## A format filed under gaming
In 2025, the French Fire and Rescue Services, working with Havas Play 🇫🇷 , ran FireCatchers — a takeover of the Twitch waiting screen, the five-to-ten-minute window before a stream begins. The screen was replaced with a live camera feed from high-risk forest areas. Viewers reported smoke in chat; corroborated reports triggered an alert to regional emergency services. The activation worked on its own terms. It used an existing media behavior and gave it a civic purpose without interrupting anything.

A Twitch waiting-screen takeover produces chat activity and viewer participation — the evidence the creator channel was built to produce — whether a commercial brand, a public agency, or no brand at all occupies the screen. The format is creator inventory by structure. The "gaming" label adds an audience descriptor and conceals the channel.

The environment did not possess the capacity to observe funnel movement to begin with. No version of this activation, run by any kind of advertiser, would change that. The disqualifier is the format, not the campaign.

## The turn
The second example is where the boundary becomes visible. The activations ran in environments where direct behavioral evidence of consumer response was structurally possible. In 2024, Renault Group placed the Renault 5 E-Tech across nine game environments — Fortnite, Minecraft, Roblox, Palworld, Subnautica, and others — through community modders, who built the car into each title in that title's own idiom. The mods were distributed through NexusMods, the Steam Workshop, and in-game codes. The activation reached players inside environments where they already were.

Several of those environments are built on live-service infrastructure that observes player behavior at every pre-conversion stage as a condition of operating. The capacity to produce direct behavioral evidence of consumer response was present in the venue. 

The activation reported none of it. The success was recorded as conversation: coverage in gaming press, spread through streamer play-throughs, volume of discussion generated outside the games. The activation ran inside environments capable of observing consumer response directly, and was measured entirely by the response that happened elsewhere.

Applying the advertising boundary test would have clarified the question some of the environments were capable of answering — did consumer response to Renault's presence occur, and was it observed where it occurred?

The mods' reported evidence was: downloads, mentions, earned coverage. None of it is direct behavioral evidence of consumer response, attributable to the brand's presence, at pre-conversion funnel stages. The venue was immersive. The output was creator and earned-media. The bundle made the substitution invisible, because under the label there was no venue and signal clarity — only "a gaming activation," reported by whatever evidence came to hand.

The advertising boundary test forces immersive environments to qualify as advertising channels. The activation surface either carries its own instrumentation capturing brand-attributed response, or it does not. The point is not that the campaign should have reported immersive evidence and failed to. The point is that no one asked whether it could, because the gaming bundle obscures the question.

## The escalation
The third activation is the largest, the most culturally ambitious, and the furthest from any qualifying environment — and the proxy it reports is the most naked of the three. In 2025, Domino's Spain marked ten years in Spanish gaming culture with Domino's Tribute: a physical monument to gaming installed in Madrid's Plaza de Callao, a launch livestream with streamers IlloJuan and Guanyar, and a documentary released on Prime Video. The livestream reached 43,000 concurrent viewers. That figure is the headline result.

That is an attendance count. It records how many people watched. It is silent on whether any of them moved one stage toward Domino's, because the channels in play — experiential, creator, branded content — do not observe that and were never built to. 

The monument is experiential marketing. The livestream is creator inventory. The documentary is branded content. Each is a real channel doing real work, and each reports its native evidence: footfall, concurrent viewers, completed views. None of it is consumer response at a pre-conversion stage, and the scale of the numbers does not change their kind. Forty-three thousand viewers is more of the proxy. It is not a different category of evidence.

The escalation is the finding. The error does not shrink as the activation grows more impressive. It grows with it. A bigger audience figure feels like better evidence. It is more of the same evidence — and the same evidence was never the kind the immersive channel is defined by.

## The missing standard
Three activations, different channels, one label. Each was planned as "gaming." None produced what the immersive channel uniquely produces, and one ran inside environments that could have, and didn't, because no test required it. The pattern is not a series of individual misjudgments. It is what planning without a standard produces, every time, by structure.

The advertising boundary test is the standard. It asks whether the environment produces direct behavioral evidence of consumer response at pre-conversion funnel stages, captured by native instrumentation, observable within a unified environment. A yes to that question earns qualification as an immersive media channel. A no is planned in its native advertising category.

Run against the test, a Twitch takeover is creator inventory and is planned as creator inventory. A modding distribution is community and earned-media inventory and is planned as such. A monument and a documentary are experiential and branded content and are planned on those terms. And an activation inside a qualifying immersive environment is recognized as structurally distinct — because it can produce response evidence the others cannot. That capacity is the only thing the "gaming" label was ever pointing at.

The bundle dissolves the moment the test is applied, because the test sorts by what each environment produces rather than by the audience it reaches. What remains is each format in its actual channel, planned on that channel's terms, and a single category (immersive) defined by its response evidence and not by its surface. The label was a substitute for that category. It is not needed once the category is named.
