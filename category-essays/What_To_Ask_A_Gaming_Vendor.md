# What To Ask A Gaming Vendor

Why the planning function was left out of gaming media, and the question that puts it back in the room.
***

Gaming arrives at a brand differently from everything else on the plan.

Almost every other channel comes through planning. Someone holds a brief, builds a plan, issues a specification, evaluates responses against it, and recommends a buy. The motion is so routine it disappears into the background. Gaming skips it. The pitch goes to the brand — to partnerships, to brand marketing, to whoever took the meeting at a conference — and the planning function finds out afterward, if at all. Where an agency is involved, it's usually inherited: a relationship that already existed, an activation already shaped, terms already agreed.

That isn't an accident of who has better relationships. Most non-endemic agencies have never bought from an endemic inventory seller, which means the function that owns evaluation in every other channel has no working experience of this one.

This is not a failure of planning. It's the absence of anything to plan on. A planner can't specify against a criterion the market hasn't published, can't compare responses that share no unit of account, and can't evaluate a claim they have no independent way to test. Faced with that, staying out of it was the correct call. The avoidance was rational, and it has been read for years as disinterest.

The cost lands in two places. The brand ends up relying on a characterization supplied by the party selling the thing — not because anyone is being deceived, but because there's no second account available and no test to run against the first. And the agency loses revenue on work it could have served, to a motion it was never invited into.

## The frame arrives with the pitch
Listen to how the offer is built. Gaming is a channel. Attention is the proof. The audience is the reason. Each of those does quiet work, and the first does the most.

A board rendered on a virtual stadium wall, a rewarded video between levels, a game streamer holding up a product, a built environment a player walks into — these carry a single audience label (gaming) yet produce entirely different things. One records that a surface was on screen. One records that a reward was claimed. One happens in a live broadcast and resolves somewhere else entirely. Grouped as a channel, they're treated as sharing a property.

That collapse is the gaming bundle: a category assembled from formats that share an audience label rather than an evidence class, held together because the distinctiveness of one member travels to all of them. It reassembles after every attempt to take it apart, and it reassembles for a reason — the member that earns the claim is still inside it.

Sort the set honestly and it comes apart on every member but one. Gaming display goes to display, gaming video to video, gaming creator content to creator, gaming sponsorship and gaming experiential to theirs, each bought on terms that already exist and already work. The sort never finishes because something is left over.

The residue is immersive media. Gaming felt like a channel because one member of it genuinely was one. Everything else was borrowing that member's distinctiveness while producing none of it.

One boundary worth setting before going further: immersive overlaps gaming media, it is not contained by it. Game environments qualify often. But nothing in the criterion below says game. An environment qualifies on what it produces, wherever it sits.

This is what makes the meeting hard to fix by good intentions alone. The bundle doesn't arrive as a claim someone has to defend; it arrives as the frame the conversation is already being held in. A planner without independent terms has no way to disagree that isn't just skepticism. Skepticism loses to a deck. Terms don't.

## Reach, attention, response
The claim that keeps gaming media bundled is that something happens inside immersive worlds that doesn't happen in a feed, and that it should be worth more. The intuition is correct. The metric chosen to express it is not.

Reach is the floor. If the message doesn't arrive, media hasn't done its most basic job. Attention grades the floor — a better-measured floor, built with real work, and still a property of the exposure rather than an observation of the consumer. Both sit on the same side of a line.

Brand-attributed response is the first observation of the consumer acting toward the brand rather than near it.

That qualifier is doing necessary work, because direct response is not new. A click is a consumer action, observed rather than inferred, and it has existed for twenty-five years. But a click is exposure plus a gesture: it records that someone was served a unit and tapped it, not that the brand's proposition was what moved them. The tap could be the mechanic, the incentive, the curiosity gap, a misclick. Near the brand is not toward the brand.

The asymmetry is structural, and it cuts both ways. A click cannot carry brand attribution even in principle, because exposure-plus-gesture is what it is by construction. Response evidence can carry it — and has to prove it, case by case, against a control. Structurally capable is not automatically so.

The obvious objection is that brands already calibrate clicks against downstream outcome. They do, constantly, and that practice has a name and an industry: multi-touch attribution and marketing-mix modeling exist precisely because clicks don't carry their own attribution and have to be reconciled to outcomes statistically, after the fact, at low confidence, in permanent dispute. The objection describes the status quo, and the status quo is the cost. Same calibration either way; one input arrives carrying its attribution, the other has to buy it back.

Here's the narrow claim: Brand-attributed response doesn't prove a sale — what happens downstream of it stays an inference, as it does in every channel. It means only that the inference begins with an observation of the consumer rather than a reconstruction of one, and that head start is the part no amount of modeling recovers afterward. Reach and attention can be measured almost anywhere. This cannot, which is a fact about environments rather than about metrics.

Which is what the residue turns out to be. Immersive isn't a format, a venue, a production budget, or a degree of visual fidelity. It is the class of environment that can produce this evidence — and that is the only thing separating it from the rest of the bundle. The other members aren't thinner versions of it, waiting for better technology or bigger budgets to catch up. They produce a different kind of thing, and always will, because the difference is structural rather than a matter of degree.

## Why attention is everywhere
Attention measurement exists because most advertisers cannot observe what happens after exposure. It is a leading indicator, sold on the promise that it predicts outcomes nobody can watch directly.

Notice what follows. Attention can be measured on display, video, social, out-of-home, CTV, and audio — anywhere there is an exposure and an eye. Its portability is not evidence of importance; it is diagnostic of what it measures. A metric that works everywhere is measuring the property all those channels share, and the property they share is that they present something and infer the rest.

So the umbrella is a symptom rather than an achievement. Attention is the best available answer to a question every channel is forced to answer indirectly — offered, increasingly, inside environments that were never forced to.

Wherever an attention claim rides on inventory inside an environment capable of observing what a person does — an owned app, a built world, anything instrumented as a condition of running — the seller has reached for the universal proxy in a venue that might not have needed it. 

Live-service game environments are the clearest case, because there the capability isn't merely possible, it's a condition of the business: a free-to-play title without pre-conversion behavioral evidence is a title that cannot operate. Reaching for the universal proxy inside the one environment class guaranteed to produce something better is the error — nothing is wrong with the proxy.

None of which makes attention data worthless. It is doing legitimate work in channels that have nothing better, and planners who use it well should keep using it. The claim is about where it sits, not what it's worth.

For a planner evaluating immersive, that tell is worth more than a critique of the seller. An attention claim riding on an environment that could observe response says two things at once: the proxy being offered is the wrong one, and the environment underneath it may be capable of more than the thing being sold. 

In the gaming bundle, however, those two facts come apart far more often than a pitch suggests — a vendor can run on instrumentation that records behavior at every stage and still be packaging a surface that records none of it. Telling the offer and the environment apart is the work.

## The question
Everything above is an argument. Here is what you can carry into a meeting with a gaming vendor offering inventory.

The planner's problem was never a shortage of opinions about gaming media. It was the absence of a test — some question whose answer doesn't depend on trusting the person asking you to buy. What follows is that test. It is one question, it is asked of the seller, and it works the same way whether the vendor is a household-name platform or someone you've never heard of, because it asks about what the environment produces rather than who is offering it.

Three things make it usable in a room. It is answerable — a vendor either can describe what their environment observes or cannot, and both are informative. It doesn't depend on your knowing the environment in advance, which matters in a category where almost no agency has bought before. And it produces the same result regardless of what the deck claims, because it is asked about the mechanics of the thing rather than about its merits.

What it gives you is a category, not a verdict on the vendor. Most of the time the answer will place the option in a channel that already has terms, prices, and a line on the plan — and putting it there is the useful outcome, not a consolation. Occasionally the answer won't fit any of them, and that is the residue this piece has been describing.

Does this environment produce direct behavioral evidence of consumer response to the brand's presence, at pre-conversion funnel stages, captured by the environment's own instrumentation, within a single evidentiary boundary?

Every qualifier is load-bearing, and each one is separately ask-able. Below, each clause is given as what you're actually probing for, what a substantive answer contains, and what the common deflection sounds like. None of these are trick questions, and none of them require technical knowledge of the environment to ask. They require only that you don't accept the bundle's framing as the terms of the conversation.

Direct — excludes inference. What a person did, observed as they did it, not modeled or surveyed afterward.

Ask: what do you observe, as opposed to what is modeled or surveyed? A substantive answer describes a record of something that happened — an action taken, at a time, by someone in the environment. The deflection is a lift study, a brand tracker, a survey panel, or a modeled result. Those aren't worthless, but they're reconstructions, and a reconstruction is what you're trying to avoid needing.

To the brand's presence — excludes a format performing well. The response has to attach to the brand being there.

Ask: what would this same thing have produced without the brand on it? A substantive answer describes some comparison where the brand is the thing that varies and everything else — the mechanic, the placement, the incentive — stays the same. The deflection is format-level performance: our units get this engagement rate, our placements get this dwell. That's a fact about the format, and it would be equally true if the brand were a blank surface.

Pre-conversion — locates the evidence where no other channel produces behavioral records at all, in the stretch between exposure and purchase.

Ask: what do you see happening between the exposure and the purchase? A substantive answer describes behavior in the middle of the funnel — someone returning, choosing, exploring, coming back a second time. The deflection runs to one end or the other: impressions at the top, conversions at the bottom. Both are already available everywhere. The middle is the part nothing else can show you.

Captured by the environment's own instrumentation — excludes measurement bolted on afterward.

Ask: what can this environment observe about behavior that is core to its operation? A substantive answer describes something the business cannot run without — a live-service funnel, a progression system, an economy that only functions because behavior is being recorded. The deflection is a list of metrics available on request, or an offer to integrate a measurement partner. Those describe a reporting layer, which is a different thing from an environment that observes as a condition of existing.

Within a single evidentiary boundary — excludes stitching.

Ask: where are those records produced — one system, or several? A substantive answer names one place where both the exposure and the response are recorded. The deflection is a description of how well the pieces connect: we can match this to your CRM, we can pass an ID, we can reconcile it after the campaign. Reconstruction doesn't become observation by being fast or by being technically impressive.

Remove any one of these five and the question starts admitting evidence some existing channel already produces. Which is why they're asked together, and why an answer that satisfies four of them is a finding rather than a pass.

## Why the conversation has to happen
A brief doesn't get you out of this. Even with an objective in hand, a budget, and a clear audience, nobody can write a specification for immersive without first learning what a specific environment can actually render and instrument. The names of the environments, the shape of the available units, what any of it can be made to observe — none of that is knowable from the outside. It comes from a conversation with someone who runs one.

Which means vendor contact isn't a step you can route around. It's a precondition. The only real question is whether the planning function is in the room when the terms get set, or whether it inherits terms someone else agreed to.

Two things worth carrying into that conversation.

Most options will route somewhere else, and that's the result working. A vendor who describes impressions, view-ability, and engagement is telling you something true and useful: this unit is display and should be bought as display, at display's price — which settles what's being sold, not what the environment could produce. That's not a rejection, and it doesn't cost you the relationship. It gives the vendor a clear answer and puts the option in the line where it can actually be evaluated. The bundle's whole function was to prevent that sentence from being sayable.

The distinction matters more than it looks. What's on the shelf and what the environment observes to stay in business are different facts, established by different questions. An operator can run on instrumentation that records behavior at every stage and still be selling you a rotating board, because the board is what's packaged and the packaging is what the pitch describes. Routing the board says nothing about the funnel underneath it. Those are the conversations most worth returning to — and closing the venue on the strength of a metrics answer is the same inheritance error the bundle runs on, pointed the other way.

A vendor who can't answer has told you something too. Nowhere in this category are these questions currently a required part of the sale. The first few people you ask will not have a prepared response, and the absence of one is itself a finding — not about their honesty, but about what the market has never been asked to produce.

Asking is a conversation. Doing it repeatably — across a set of vendors, in a way that turns answers into decisions someone else can check — is a practice, and the market doesn't have one yet.

Reach is the floor. Attention grades the floor. What justifies a brand's presence in these environments was never the exposure — it is whether anyone can show what the presence moved.
