# Wendy's RPG Answered a Different Brief

Building a brand game is hard. It is not too hard. A brand has to pick the right objective, then design for it.

launched [WendyQuest: Legend of the Four Corner Stones](https://nerdist.com/article/wendys-wendyquest-free-rpg-win-rewards/) on November 4. It is a free retro roleplaying game (**RPG**) developed by 81Monkeys: ~15 hours long, on Steam, iOS and Android. You play as Wendy. The villains wear a golden "M." Each boss you beat unlocks a code for real food in the Wendy's app.

Here's a question many in the gaming industry are asking: **Is Steam the right place for it?** A standalone game has to fight for every download, while Roblox and Fortnite already have the players. On November 8, SteamDB recorded an all-time peak of 780 players in the game at once, set that day, and 278 user reviews, and Steam rated the reviews 92% positive. The people who found it love it. Have enough people found it?

That is a fair question about one objective. **It is the wrong question for deciding whether a brand should build its own game.**

## The download question scores one objective

The critique judges the game on reach. Peak players, reviews and downloads all count how many people showed up. On that count, a standalone game on Steam loses to a branded experience on a platform that already has the audience. The critique is spot on there.

But reach is one objective among several, and a brand rarely builds a game for reach alone. Reach is the cheapest thing a brand can buy. Paid social, creator deals and in-game display deliver it at scale, for far less than a 15-hour RPG.

So "is Steam the right place?" has no answer until a second question is settled: what is the game for? Each objective has its own measure. A game can do well on one and be the wrong tool for another. The mistake is to judge every brand game on the lowest hanging measure.

## What WendyQuest does well

Judged on the right objectives, WendyQuest is a good build. It serves two of them well and one in part.

![Article content](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D4E12AQHDWVt2FF7Lhw/article-inline_image-shrink_1000_1488/B4EaHCIYMEIgAM-/0/1794258339127?e=1795651200&v=beta&t=sCm6JDKdHbUTdw-KQvBW4Gnij0MnKUU974Q7ikDEzsk)

The loyalty row deserves more credit than it will get. The redemptions land in Wendy's own app, not on a platform dashboard. That is a brand owned record. Most brand-furnished games do not produce that.

The press row is real too. Wendy's made the brand the whole world, cast its rival as the villain and made the punchline the game. That is memorable. A smaller role for the brand might have been less so.

## The objective it cannot serve

The last row is the one no other brand line delivers on its own: *did the brand's presence move the behavior of the people it reached toward the brand, before the sale, with no offer or reward?* WendyQuest cannot answer that, for two reasons.

**The reward pays for the choice.** When a boss win unlocks free food, players have a reason to engage that has nothing to do with the brand (who doesn't love free food?). Engagement then shows response to the offer, not to Wendy's. The reward is what makes the loyalty row work, and it is what rules out this objective.

**The brand is the whole world.** You play Wendy. Every region, ally and joke is Wendy's. There is no other option a player can pick instead, so no one can show they preferred the brand. Rivals appear only as enemies. And there is no way to compare the game with the same activation without the brand.

The reward could be removed in a later version. The brand-centric structure could not, without rebuilding the game. That is the real lesson of WendyQuest. It was not the wrong bet. It was designed for press and loyalty, and the design closes off the objective that immersive media, bought right, exists to serve.

## What the boundary asks of an owned game

An owned game can serve the objective — moving behavior of the people it reached toward the brand, before sale, with no offer or reward. To do so, however, it must meet the boundary that defines immersive media bought right. None of the conditions is unusual. Each is a design choice made before the build starts.

- **The brand plays a role, not the whole world.** It can be a faction, an ally, an item, an ability or a place. It can be the most visible thing in the game. It cannot be the only option.
- **Other options sit next to it.** Players can choose the brand or something else in the same role. The chance that they pass is what makes it count when they don't.
- **The same activation can run without the brand.** Players are split between the branded version and an unbranded one, with matched traffic. Because the brand is one role, an unbranded version can be placed in the same role.
- **No reward is attached to the brand.** No price, discount or prize acts on choosing it. Rewards can live elsewhere in the brand's plan, on their own line.
- **The brand can read the record.** It reaches the brand without a platform or vendor in between.
- **The game is a standing property.** It is built to outlive one campaign — the properties of a qualifying build are reused.

The last condition answers the download question. A one-campaign game carries its whole build cost on one launch, so reach is all it can be judged on. A standing game spreads that cost over every campaign it carries. A brand with one campaign and this objective should not build a game at all. It should put its presence into a world someone else runs.

## What the brand gets

With immersive media bought right, the buy places your brand where people can choose it or refuse it. Its own record shows whether, and how far, it moved the behavior of the people it reached toward your brand, with no offer or reward attached, before the sale.

That is one event, not three. Today a brand pays for the activation, pays again for media to carry it, and often pays a third time for a survey or lift study that asks people afterwards what they think/how they feel. Here the activation is the media, and the record comes with it.

The record can come back flat. A record that can say no is worth taking to finance. The same record can show where the pull stopped. Players may notice the brand but not come back. They may come back but not pick it over the other options. Each points to a different fix. Later buys can redesign the brand's role and read again. That is the lever game design gives a brand, and a standing world is the surface to use it.

None of this costs the brand the other rows. A qualifying game still makes news, and it can still feed a loyalty program on a separate line. Those come along, each read in its own column. They just do not decide what the game is for.

## Pick the objective, then the design

The question for a brand is not "can we get enough downloads?" It is "what is this game for?"

- If it is for press, build what Wendy's built. It worked.
- If it is for loyalty, attach the reward and read the redemptions in your own app. That worked too.
- If it is for moving people's behavior toward the brand, design for it from the start: the brand in a role, other options next to it, the same activation without the brand, no reward or offer, a record the brand can read, and a world that outlives the campaign.

A branded game is hard, yes. Too hard, no — if the game serves the right objective. WendyQuest is a good game built for the objectives it serves. The next brand to build one has a choice Wendy's did not make: build a game that can show whether the brand moved anyone.
