# The Gaming Label Failed It's Last Job
 
Bain & Company just told gaming CEOs that the average gamer was always a fiction. The report is about which games to build. It settles something about which media to buy.
***
 
The Gaming Marketing Institute, which I founded, has published a series of pieces making one central argument: gaming is not a channel. It is an audience label attached to a collection of formats that share nothing but the people they reach. Display in a mobile title, a sponsored stream, an esports activation, and a branded in-game integration produce different evidence, on different terms, captured by different infrastructure. Sort them by what they produce and the bundle comes apart.
 
That argument dissolved the label from the format side. It left one thing standing: the audience. The formats shared nothing, but the label still seemed to name a coherent population — the gamer — and a coherent population is a market a planner can size and aim at. That was the label's last job.
 
Bain's 2026 Gaming Report, published August 18, removes it. 
 
## What Bain found
Bain surveyed more than 5,300 gamers worldwide and asked them to describe the gaming experience they want most. The answers split five ways, and no single experience type surpassed 26%. Story-driven games took the top slot at just over a quarter. Open sandbox and user-generated worlds took 22%. A fifth said it depends on their mood. Multiplayer competition — the experience most people picture when they picture a gamer — came last, at 15%.
 
The split sharpens by cohort. A third of 13-to-17-year-olds want open, user-generated worlds. Among players 50 and older that share falls to 15% or lower, and among players in their 70s it is 3%. Preference for the same experience runs from 31% in Brazil to 11% in Japan.
 
Bain's conclusion, addressed to studio leadership: "The average gamer was always a convenient fiction." The prescription that follows is to stop sizing the market by its mean and start sizing it by the characteristics that actually split it — age, geography, taste, spending tier, creative identity — and to understand players through their behavior rather than their demographics.
 
## Both halves of the label are now empty
A category label can do one of two jobs. It can name a property the things inside it share, or it can name a population the things inside it reach. Gaming was never able to do the first. The formats under it share no evidence class, no buying motion, and no measurement standard. That is why gaming media budgets don't exist: the spend books to display, paid social, and sponsorship, on those channels' terms, and no ledger anywhere carries a gaming line.
 
The label survived on the second job. Even if "gaming" named no channel, it seemed to name a person — young, engaged, digitally native, wanting a recognizable set of things. A planner could plan against that person even without a channel to plan in.
 
Bain shows there is no such person. The population under the label wants opposite things, in proportions that shift by age and country, with no experience commanding even a plurality. An interest tag can still be sold against this population, and it still clears — Meta sells one every day. But a tag is reach, not a market. What Bain removes is the market behind the tag: the idea that "gamers" describes a demand you can size, price, and aim at as one thing.
 
A label that names neither a shared property nor a shared preference has nothing left to name. The format side was settled by what the formats produce. The audience side is now settled by what the audience wants. The label is empty on both.
 
## What this report does not say
Bain fragments the games business. Its subject is what players want from games, and its audience is the studios deciding which games to build and where to put capital. It says nothing about gaming media, nothing about which advertising surfaces qualify as a channel, and nothing about the immersive media advertising boundary test: 
 
Does an environment produces direct behavioral evidence of consumer response at pre-conversion funnel stages, captured by native instrumentation, attributable to the brand's presence? That question is not in the report, and this piece does not put it there.
 
The report's evidence class settles the same point from the other direction. Bain's findings are survey findings — stated preference and self-reported spend. Immersive media's advertising boundary excludes stated evidence from channel qualification. As a result, this report can support a claim about what the audience is. The grounds to qualify a single environment as an immersive media channel is a different thing altogether.
 
## The numbers a vendor will quote next
Two figures in the report will appear in gaming-media decks before the quarter ends. The most engaged 20% of gamers account for 59% of total playing time, and the top 20% of spenders account for 73% of total spending. Players who create game content for public use are twice as likely to spend money at all.
 
The pitch those figures will be made to carry is already written: the valuable players are concentrated and they are inside games, so pay a premium for gaming media to reach them. That pitch fails on a distinction the audience series already established. The scarcity around gaming audiences was never in the person. It was in the context.

Bain's concentration describes who spends on games. It says nothing about who is scarce to advertising. A top-20% spender scrolls the same feed as everyone else, watches the same pre-roll, and passes the same retargeted banner, and is targetable in all three places at commodity rates as a gaming-interest segment. 
 
Concentration of spend on games does not make the spender hard to reach with media. In other words, the figures describe contextual value, not elusiveness — and endemic (gaming) media is priced on elusiveness.
 
The associated premium (paying to reach an elusive audience) prices the label. Bain just showed the label is empty. A number pulled from a report that retires the average gamer cannot be used to charge more for reaching him.
 
## The report is not a case for better targeting
The second reading is already in the market, and it arrives with a better pedigree than the first. It says: Bain has shown the audience fragments along lines a media plan can't see, so what the plan needs is gaming data — behavioral segments that resolve "gamer" into the cohorts, like those Bain outlined. Better data, more precise targeting, the label rescued at higher resolution.
 
That reading fails before it reaches the plan, because it mistakes what Bain's variables predict. Bain's segments are built to predict game demand: which experience a player will buy, how much they will spend on it, whether they will make content for it. A studio needs that prediction because a studio sells games. A non-endemic brand does not. Nothing in the report shows that a story-driven player in Japan responds to a car ad differently from a sandbox player in Brazil, because the report never asked. Resolving the label into finer slices of game preference produces more precise data about the wrong outcome.
 
Suppose the data were relevant. It would still be solving the problem this category never had. Targeting answers who and where. The audience series settled both: the person is reachable today, at scale, on every conventional channel, and the platforms already sell the interest and behavioral segments that prove it. A gaming-behavior segment appended to a paid social buy is a paid social buy with one more data line. It changes nothing about what the impression produces.
 
That is the distinction the refrain conceals. Targeting data is an input applied before exposure. The evidence a channel is qualified on is a record produced after it. No amount of precision about who was reached changes the record of whether they responded. Better data about the person cannot manufacture response evidence.
 
Read the two readings together and they are one reading. The endemic premium prices the person as scarce. The data pitch prices the person as knowable. Both sell the person. Bain says the person does not exist as a single thing, and the audience series says he was never the scarce thing. A vendor holding this report is holding a document that retires the product.
 
## Where Bain's signal actually lives
The report's prescription is to sort players by what they do. Read the behaviors Bain uses to do it: hours played, in-game spend, content created for public use. Every one of those is traces to exactly one place — inside the environment, backed by instrumentation the environment runs to operate at all.
 
That is the same class of signal the immersive channel is defined by. A free-to-play live-service environment observes activation, engagement, retention, and monetization conversion natively, because its commercial model depends on observing them. Bain, working on product strategy, arrived at behavioral sorting independently and then located the sorting signal in the one place where that evidence is produced as a condition of operating.
 
The report does not notice this. It is not a media report and had no reason to. But the implication holds without Bain's endorsement: the market's own method for finding the valuable player runs on evidence that exists only in-environment. The person is reachable anywhere. The identifying signal is not.
 
This is also why the signal cannot be exported as a targeting product. Hours, spend, and creation are outcome records — they indicate what a player did inside a world. Package them as a segment and ship them to a DSP, and they become an audience descriptor: a statement about who someone probably is, inferred from where they have been. 
 
The same class of evidence exists on social and video platforms, at scale. The move that turns Bain's signal into a data product is the move that strips it of standing. Inside the environment it is evidence of response. Outside it, it is another label with more decimal places.
 
## The finding
Gaming is not a channel, and as of this month it is not an audience either — not in the sense a planner needs, a population that wants one thing and can be sized as one demand. The label did two jobs badly and has now been relieved of both. The formats return to the channels their evidence places them in. 
 
The people return to the interest segments the platforms already sell. Neither a premium on reaching them nor a data product for finding them restores what the label lost, because both price the person, and the person was never the scarce thing.
 
What remains is what always remained after the sort: the immersive context where attention demonstrably occurs, where the behavior the market uses to find value is natively observed, and where nothing has historically been buyable in a distinct channel. Bain wrote a report about game design and confirmed the audience half of that argument in eight words. The channel half still has no line in any book.
