# Gaming's CTV Comparison Is Right

The industry's best argument for gaming's funding gap is that connected TV (**CTV**) had the same gap and closed it. That is true, and it predicts the opposite of what it is offered for.

---

At IAB PlayFronts 2026, the keynote made the case in one comparison. Connected TV took 20.2% of American media time in 2025 and 7.7% of ad spend [1]. The gap is closing and the channel is mainstream. Gaming, the argument runs, has the same shape of problem, just earlier on the curve [2].

The comparison is strong. Every other claim in the pitch is endemic: audience size, engagement, attention scores. The CTV comparison is a precedent from mainstream media that mainstream planners watched happen. **It is the one argument a non-endemic planner already believes**, which is why it deserves investigation.

## How CTV's gap closed

CTV's gap did not close because time spent was large. Streaming hours were large for years while television money stayed in linear. The hours were the reason a planner looked. They were not the reason money moved.

Money moved when the inventory became one unit. The Media Rating Council (MRC) finalized cross-media measurement standards for TV and video in September 2019, covering broadcast, cable and streaming on the same terms [3]. It issued CTV-specific measurement guidelines in August 2021 [4], and the first MRC accreditation for CTV viewable impressions followed in 2023 [5].

On the buying side, Disney's 2020 upfront was the first where Hulu sold inside the Disney portfolio: one deal, one price, guaranteed on completed views, running anywhere across the digital estate [6].

At that point a planner could enumerate streaming inventory across publishers, qualify one publisher's inventory against another's, compare two prices, and write a specification a second seller had to fill. **CTV closed its gap by becoming a line inside the video budget.** It stopped being a category and became a unit.

That is the precedent, in a nutshell. Share of time was necessary and insufficient. A legible unit was what unlocked spend.

## Spend follows the unit, not the time

The pattern is not specific to television. Podcasts carried large audiences for a decade while host-reads were negotiated show by show and reported by the host. Brand spend stayed small until certified downloads made the inventory comparable and dynamic insertion made it specifiable. The format never changed. The unit did, and the money followed it.

Creator scaled the same way with no trade body behind it. Platforms enumerated creators, data vendors qualified them, cost-per-post compared them, and codes and links specified what a brand wanted and verified what came back.

Retail media moved out of trade budgets when it became priced delivery. Esports is the confirming failure, in gaming: it had the audience argument in full and never produced a unit a buyer could compare, so it earned sponsorship money and hit the ceiling sponsorship always hits. **In every case the audience was real before the money moved, and the money moved when the unit arrived.**

## Run the precedent on gaming

Apply CTV's history to gaming and the first thing it does is sort the gaming media bundle.

Mobile display and rewarded video are the bulk of what the industry counts as gaming ad spend. They are already video's unit. They clear through video exchanges, count to video standards, and sit on the video and display lines of every brand that buys them.

The IAB's Intrinsic In-Game Measurement Guidelines, released with the MRC in 2022, state their purpose as bringing in-game ads up to par with the rest of digital media [7]. In other words, the standardisation work underway is doing for in-game display and video exactly what it did for CTV: absorbing it into the line that already had rules. Where anyone looking for a gaming line won't find one.

A stream sponsorship is creator inventory and books as creator. A Twitch takeover is display and video against a Twitch audience and books as such. A jersey patch is sponsorship. Each of these already has a unit, a rule, and a home. None of them needs a gaming budget, and none of them will get one, because budgets follow channels and each of these is already in its channel.

What remains after the sort is one thing. Certain immersive environments produce direct behavioral evidence of consumer response to the brand's presence, at pre-conversion funnel stages, captured by the environment's native instrumentation, inside a unified environment. Display cannot produce it. Video cannot produce it. Sponsorship cannot produce it. **It is the only part of gaming that cannot be absorbed by an existing line, and it is the only part with no unit, no rule, and no line of its own.** CTV's precedent locates the missing budget exactly there.

## What the gap figure is measuring

Likewise, the gap figure is an artifact of its own construction. The spend share the industry cites is eMarketer's: in-game advertising at 2.3% of US digital ad spending in 2026 [8]. That is a share of digital spend, not of media spend, and it counts ads served inside games. It does not include Twitch, creator, and social spend against gaming audiences. The comparison is a channel share against a consumer interest figure. The same construction would show that food advertising is underfunded against the share of Americans who eat.

The forecast behind the number says two more things the keynote left out. Most in-game ad spend goes to mobile, and most of the ads are bought by game developers [9]: the numerator is largely games advertising games to players. Meanwhile, gaming's share of digital ad spend is falling, to 2% by 2029 on eMarketer's own projection [8]. **The industry's preferred forecaster says the gap is widening, and says it is widening in inventory that already has a home.**

That does not make the gap fictional. It makes it two gaps. One is a distribution lag on inventory that already has a unit, and it is closing the way CTV's closed, into the lines that own the unit. The other is a spend with no line at all: the in-game brand activation, commissioned as a one-off partnership, funded from experiential and innovation budgets, and reported as engagement counts. **The first gap needs no gaming standard, and the second cannot close without its own standard.**

## A prediction, marked as one

The CTV comparison, offered as a forecast, cannot be wrong. Any year the money does not arrive is a year that was "earlier on the curve."

The unit reading makes a prediction that can fail. So, here it is: gaming ad revenue will keep reporting against display, video, creator, and sponsorship lines, measured to those channels' standards and bought on those channels' motions. Growth will be growth of those lines into gaming audiences. If a major platform or holding company reports a gaming line, measured to a gaming standard and bought on a gaming motion, this reading is wrong.

If it does not happen, the CTV precedent is the reason. CTV did not get a CTV line. It got a video line with more inventory on it. **Gaming will end the way CTV ended, absorbed into the lines that already had rules**, with one exception: the format that produces evidence no existing line can hold. That format closes its gap the day it gets a unit, and not before.

## The finding

The comparison is correct. Gaming is CTV. Read the precedent thoroughly and it says that share of time draws a look, a unit draws a budget, and a category with no unit becomes a line inside someone else's. The industry is standardizing the parts of gaming that already had homes. The part with no home is the one part the standardization does not touch.
