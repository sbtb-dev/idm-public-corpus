One question belongs in every meeting where Electronic Arts (EA) prices its in-game inventory, and it is neither hostile nor rhetorical. It is due diligence, the same instinct any buyer applies to any vendor: does the seller run its own product?

EA Advertising, launched in June 2026, sells rotating digital ad boards, scoreboards, and broadcast overlays across Madden, College Football, and EA SPORTS FC to Visa, Lowe's Companies, Inc., Red Bull, Xfinity, Peacock, and Mountain Dew. So the brand across the table should ask, before anything is signed: is EA buying the billboards it sells — not from a rival, from itself?

## The Market's Cleanest Test Sits in EA's Own Catalog
EA owns the single best test of intrinsic in-game advertising available anywhere: a Madden billboard rotating inside College Football's stadiums. Every condition is maximal. Domain fit — EA itself treats the two titles as one funnel, selling their Deluxe Editions together in an MVP Bundle and advertising both games in a single TV spot.

Cost is near zero. The ad server EA built into its Frostbite engine already rotates boards through College Football's stadiums; pointing one at Madden is a scheduling decision, not an engineering project. Rivalry — none; no competitor's platform gets validated.

The measurement is without equal anywhere in advertising. The same player account sits on both sides of the exposure, first-party, resolved all the way to purchase, with far less privacy friction than any outside buyer will ever face. The companion piece, EA Is Selling Impressions While Sitting on Response, reads that instrumentation in full; here it needs one sentence — if the billboard moves anyone toward anything, this is where the movement is visible.

## What the Record Shows, and What It Doesn't
I won't claim to know whether that rotation exists. Nothing public settles it, and the honest position is to say so. The public record does show where EA's cross-title promotion visibly runs: retail bundles, pre-order incentives, storefront placement, joint television spots — units that return receipts, purchases and pre-orders countable by design.

No coverage, sales material, or announcement describes the in-world board carrying EA's own titles. That is silence, not proof. But the question resolves in one meeting, because the seller knows the answer, and both answers are findings.

## Both Answers Are Findings
If the answer is no, then the operator holding a free test, perfect fit, and total measurement never pointed its own unit at its own product — and every brand buying the board is funding a claim its seller could have checked at a price of zero and didn't.

If the answer is yes, existence alone settles nothing, because installing a house ad in your own rotation is free and proves only that someone scheduled it. So the diligence continues, and the brand should ask what any procurement team asks about a vendor's own usage: what evidence the placement produced of moving players toward Madden, how much of the audience it reached was already Madden's, and whether EA would make the same buy on a client's budget at the client's price.

If EA answers with evidence, the case for this unit finally exists — and EA should be leading every pitch with it, because no attention study could compete with that slide. If EA installed the board and never measured it, the brand has learned the category's actual norm: the unit goes unexamined even where examination was nearly free.

## The Premium Tier Sharpens the Question
Mountain Dew's DEW University is a fully playable branded team inside College Football, with its own stadium and mascot. It sits inside a live-service environment that watches player behavior continuously as a condition of operating. This is the one class of venue in advertising capable of observing response before conversion.

Now read what EA reports from other similar placements: 128 million matches played against Red Bull's integration, 200,000 challenge completions for Lowe's, impressions verified for viewability by Integral Ad Science. Activity counts and exposure standards — evidence that activity happened, not evidence of what it moved. The venue could produce more. The reporting standard never asks it to, because nowhere in this category are those three questions a required deliverable of the sale.

## The Specimen, Not the Exception
None of this is misconduct. Both sides of EA's behavior are rational at every step: sell the board to buyers who will pay for exposure; promote your own titles through bundles and pre-orders that return receipts. Correct move, correct move.

What the two correct moves expose, held in one hand, is the standard the market lets the sale clear on — loose enough that the seller's own answer to this question has never been part of any sales conversation. The audit travels to every endemic seller with a sibling product and an idle slot in its own rotation.

## Ask It Anyway
The question costs the brand nothing — no analyst, no study, one sentence in a meeting the brand is already sitting in. And whichever answer comes back, it returns the same finding: what justifies a presence in these environments was never the exposure. It is whether anyone can show what the exposure moved. The seller knows whether it ever looked. The brand should ask.
