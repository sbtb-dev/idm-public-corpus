# Roblox Priced The Wrong Unit

A note on Roblox's new CPM framework — and why a premium impression has no scaling story.
***

When I read that Roblox had set CPM-based fees for brand integrations, the first question I had wasn't about the rate. It was simpler, and it's the question every brand/agency should be asking: 

Why pay a premium for an impression here when impressions are cheaper everywhere else?
The honest answer is worse than "cheaper elsewhere." An impression-priced integration doesn't just lose a price comparison. It lands in the one position on the scaling test that structurally cannot scale: high operational cost paired with commodity output. Pricing the integration on CPM takes the format's worst feature — its cost — and meters it against its weakest output — the impression. That isn't a thin scaling justification. It's a negative one.

## The commodity-pricing problem
An impression is a commodity, and a commodity clears at the price of its cheapest abundant supply. The moment the unit of account is the impression, the comparison set is every impression-producing channel — programmatic display, social, in-app — where impressions are bought at scale, self-serve, frictionless. 

A buyer evaluating on consistent terms will not pay a premium for a unit they can source cheaper in volume. So a premium impression has to carry something the cheap impression doesn't, or its premium is indefensible. The premium has to be earned on the unit, and the impression cannot earn it.

To clarify, Roblox's headline CPM isn't high in isolation — $1.50 is below social CPMs. The cost problem is that this CPM is a fee layered on top of a custom integration the brand already pays a creator to build, plus the Portal traffic spend that generates the impressions in the first place. The fully-loaded cost per impression of a branded integration is high relative to commodity supply, even when the headline rate looks low. The comparison that matters is fully-loaded, and on that basis the buyer's instinct holds.

## Why it's worse than a price comparison
An advertising format scales in proportion to the ratio between two variables: what it costs to acquire and operate the inventory, and what brand-attributed response evidence it produces. Display scales as commodity inventory because it's low input cost with modeled output — volume compensates for per-unit weakness. A branded integration sits at the opposite corner on the cost axis. It's a custom build with partnership complexity and production overhead. It carries the input profile of an experiential activation.

That places the impression-priced integration in the quadrant where strong friction meets weak categorical output. Weak evidence at high operational cost does not scale — the format cannot justify its friction without a commensurate evidentiary return. So the CPM doesn't merely fail to help. It actively pins a high-cost format to a low-value output class, which is the precise combination that has no path to scale. The thing that could justify the cost — evidence no other channel produces — is the thing a CPM does not price.

## Defusing the steelman
Three defenses of the premium impression exist. Each fails on a different axis.

The first is audience scarcity: the gaming cohort is hard to reach, so the impression is worth more. It isn't. The audience is reachable everywhere except in-environment — the person is trivially reachable in a feed for pennies; only the in-world context was never sellable. The scarcity is context-scarcity, not person-scarcity. And filling the scarce context with a commodity impression spends the scarcity premium on the one unit that doesn't need the scarce context to exist. You paid for the place and produced the thing you could have produced anywhere.

The second is ad-avoidance: hard-to-reach cohorts block ads, so in-environment reach is uniquely valuable. The avoidance is a cohort trend, not a Roblox property — the same demographic avoids ads identically whether or not you tag them gamers. So the avoidance argues against pouring more into the channels where reach is already solved. However, it doesn't convert an in-game impression into something other than an impression.

The third is the strongest: attention. In-environment attention is higher-quality, so the premium is an attention premium, not a reach premium. Grant the attention fully — lower fatigue, higher dwell, less avoidance. The effectiveness is unattributed. Higher attention to the ad unit is still attention to the unit, not consumer response attributable to the brand. It belongs to the format, not to the brand. 

So the most defensible version of the premium is an attention buy — and attention is itself a contested, separately-priced proxy that competes against cheaper attention elsewhere and is measured by third parties. Even at its best, the premium impression is competing inside the impression-and-attention market as a high-cost entrant. It is not establishing a channel. The conclusion survives every defense: impressions don't justify scale, and premium impressions justify it least of all because they invite the comparison they lose.

## The ceiling, stated in budget terms
There's a second way to see the ceiling, and precision matters here, because brands scale impression buying every day. Commodity impressions scale because they're cost effective: low cost lets volume compensate for weak per-unit evidence, and the buy clears on modeled efficiency. That route is open to display and paid social. It is closed to a premium integration — because expense cannot be compensated by volume, and high input cost is the one variable scale cannot dilute.

So a premium format carries an expansion burden the commodity formats don't: it has to justify its cost, and the only thing that justifies it is the response evidence it would produce that nothing else does — the evidence that earns the next allocation. 

Price the integration on impressions and that justification disappears. It produces an exposure count, the exposure count is available cheaper elsewhere, and the premium is left with nothing to point to that would defend scaling it. 

A premium format that dissolves into buying impressions can be scaled as neither commodity nor performance — too expensive for the first, evidentiary-empty for the second. What remains is a discretionary brand line, capped at what leadership will defend on strategic grounds. That's the scaling ceiling, expressed as a budget fact rather than a media one.

## What would actually justify scale
The only scaling justification an immersive media format has is the evidence no other channel can produce — direct behavioral response to the brand's presence, at pre-conversion funnel stages, captured by the environment's native instrumentation. Strong evidence at high operational cost scales selectively: the friction is justified because the evidentiary return is unavailable anywhere else. That is the only quadrant a custom integration can win from, and it's the quadrant the impression unit forecloses.

So the deepest read isn't that Roblox priced integrations too high. It's that Roblox priced it on the wrong axis entirely. The integration's claim to a premium — to any scaling story at all — lives in the response evidence. Metering it by impression concedes, in the unit of account itself, that the brand is buying exposure. And the moment the buyer is paying for exposure, the question answers itself: they will buy it cheaper somewhere else, and they should. The justification for scale was never the impression. It was the evidence the impression isn't.
